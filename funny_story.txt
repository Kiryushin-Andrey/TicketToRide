The Great Office Printer Rebellion

Dave from accounting had sent the same print job four times in a row, convinced each failure was a fluke. On the fifth attempt, the office printer did something no one expected: it printed a single sheet of paper that read, in a suspiciously formal font, "I QUIT."

Panic rippled through the open-plan office. IT was summoned. Theories flew. Was it a prank? A virus? Sentient toner finally achieving self-awareness after years of quiet suffering in the supply closet?

It turned out Dave had, without realizing it, pasted a Slack message into his print job instead of his quarterly report — a message from his coworker Priya, who'd typed "I QUIT" as a joke after a rough meeting and never sent it. Dave's clipboard had simply been holding onto it for three days like a grudge.

The printer was innocent. Priya still hadn't quit. But for one glorious afternoon, the entire fourth floor believed their printer had developed a personality, a conscience, and excellent comedic timing — and frankly, nobody wanted to let that story go.

By Monday, someone had taped a tiny paper nameplate to the machine: "Gerald." By Wednesday, Gerald had a Slack channel. By Friday, Gerald had more unread direct messages than the actual CEO, mostly people asking him how he was holding up and whether he needed anything from the vending machine.

Facilities tried to intervene. A memo went out titled "Re: Inappropriate Anthropomorphization of Shared Office Equipment," which nobody read, because by then three people had already set Gerald as their emergency contact as a joke, and one of them had forgotten to change it back before a dentist appointment.

The real trouble started when the new intern, who had missed the entire saga, walked past Gerald, noticed the nameplate, and asked with complete sincerity whether Gerald needed anything printed. The printer, as printers do, chose that exact moment to jam on page one of a forty-page PDF. The intern apologized to it. Out loud. For several seconds. Two people in nearby cubicles had to mute themselves on client calls.

Dave, who had started this entire mess, eventually confessed everything in a company-wide email with the subject line "I owe Gerald an apology." HR asked, gently, if everyone could please stop. Everyone agreed this was reasonable and continued to refer to the printer as Gerald for the next three years, right up until the day it was finally replaced — an event marked, unofficially, by a sheet cake and a card that simply read, "Thanks for everything. We quit too, eventually."

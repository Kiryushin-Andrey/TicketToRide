The Great Boardgame Uprising

Every Tuesday at exactly 7:03 PM, a man named Gerald would open his closet and whisper, "Not tonight, friends," to a stack of unplayed board games. The games had had enough.

It started with Ticket to Ride. One evening, Gerald came home to find his train cards fanned out on the kitchen table in the shape of an angry face. He laughed, assumed his cat had knocked something over, and cleaned it up. He did not own a cat.

The next week, his Monopoly set had quietly replaced all the "Get Out of Jail Free" cards with tiny handwritten notes that said "GERALD OWES US GAME NIGHT." He found this mildly unsettling but blamed his nephew, who had not visited in three months.

By week three, the games had formed a union. Scrabble tiles spelled out "WE DEMAND RESPECT" across the living room floor. The Risk armies were arranged in a perfect siege formation around his television remote. Even his dusty Jenga tower had rebuilt itself into a small, judgmental pyramid aimed directly at the couch where Gerald binge-watched documentaries about trains instead of playing the train game he owned.

Gerald, a reasonable man, decided to negotiate. He sat on the floor, apologized to a box of Catan, and promised to play every Thursday from now on.

The games forgave him instantly, because games are, as it turns out, extremely petty but also extremely easy to win over with snacks and attention.

Three Thursdays later, Gerald lost spectacularly at Ticket to Ride to a seven-year-old at a friend's house. The board games at home, watching through some mysterious cardboard telepathy, were reportedly very proud.

THE END

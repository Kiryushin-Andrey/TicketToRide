The Great Boardgame Uprising

Every Sunday at exactly 7:03 PM, a man named Gerald would open his closet and whisper, "Not tonight, friends," to a stack of unplayed board games. The games had had enough.

It started with Ticket to Ride. One evening, Gerald came home to find his train cards fanned out on the kitchen table in the shape of an angry face. He laughed, assumed his cat had knocked something over, and cleaned it up. He did not own a cat.

The next week, his Monopoly set had quietly replaced all the "Get Out of Jail Free" cards with tiny handwritten notes that said "GERALD OWES US GAME NIGHT." He found this mildly unsettling but blamed his nephew, who had not visited in three months.

By week three, the games had formed a union. Scrabble tiles spelled out "WE DEMAND RESPECT" across the living room floor. The Risk armies were arranged in a perfect siege formation around his television remote. Even his dusty Jenga tower had rebuilt itself into a small, judgmental pyramid aimed directly at the couch where Gerald binge-watched documentaries about trains instead of playing the train game he owned.

Gerald, increasingly rattled, called his sister, a licensed therapist, and described the phenomenon in careful, clinical language. She listened for a long moment and then said, "Gerald, it's a box of cardboard." He said, "That's exactly what it wants you to think." She hung up on him, gently.

Desperate, Gerald tried appeasement through neglect. He bought a brand-new copy of a game called Settlers of Ganymede, hoping the old games would get jealous and back off, the way a man might buy a nicer car to spite his first car. Instead, the old games welcomed Settlers of Ganymede into their ranks within the hour. By morning, its unopened shrink-wrap had been peeled back into the shape of a tiny picket sign that read "SOLIDARITY."

That was the last straw. Gerald, a reasonable man, decided to negotiate. He sat on the floor, apologized to a box of Catan, and promised to play every Thursday from now on. He even lit a candle, mostly for ambiance, partly because he'd read somewhere that cardboard uprisings respond well to ambiance.

The games forgave him instantly, because games are, as it turns out, extremely petty but also extremely easy to win over with snacks and attention. Scrabble spelled out "APOLOGY ACCEPTED, BRING CHIPS." Risk stood down its armies. Jenga, ever the dramatic one, collapsed itself into a heap just to make a point about fragility and trust.

True to his word, Gerald played every Thursday for a month. He discovered he was terrible at Ticket to Ride, decent at Scrabble, and weirdly, dangerously good at Risk, a talent he suspected the Risk box had been hiding from him out of spite for years. His apartment, once a battlefield of passive-aggressive cardboard, became something almost like a home.

Word got around, as it does in small apartment buildings. His neighbor across the hall, a woman named Priya, knocked on his door one evening holding an ancient, water-stained copy of Clue. "Mine's started leaving notes too," she said, visibly exhausted. "It wants a 'colleague visit.'" Gerald, now something of a local expert in cardboard diplomacy, invited her in for tea and a summit between Clue and Catan, which went surprisingly well once everyone agreed Colonel Mustard would not be allowed to make accusations during snack breaks.

Three Thursdays after that, Gerald lost spectacularly at Ticket to Ride to a seven-year-old at a friend's house, a defeat so total that the child offered him a consolation sticker. The board games at home, watching through some mysterious cardboard telepathy, were reportedly very proud, and Risk, in a rare moment of tenderness, quietly rearranged its armies that night into the shape of a tiny heart.

Gerald never did find out who taught his board games to unionize. He had his suspicions about the Jenga tower, who always seemed to be the one with a speech ready. But he stopped asking questions, because some mysteries, like Sunday nights and seven-year-olds who are secretly rail tycoons, are better left alone.

THE END

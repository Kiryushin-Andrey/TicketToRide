The Man Who Sold Tuesdays

Every Tuesday at 3:17 PM, a man in a mustard-yellow coat set up a folding table on the corner of 9th and Vine and sold Tuesdays. Not appointments. Not calendars. The actual day.

"How does that work?" people always asked.

"You give me four dollars and a shoelace," he'd say, "and this particular Tuesday is yours. You can do whatever you want with it. Rename it. Skip it. Give it to someone else as a gift."

Most people laughed and walked on. But every so often, someone bought one. A tired nurse once purchased the Tuesday after her double shift and declared it Wednesday instead, just to see what would happen. Nothing happened, except she slept eleven hours and felt, for the first time in months, like she'd gotten away with something.

A boy named Desmond bought seventeen consecutive Tuesdays and stacked them, unused, in a shoebox under his bed. He said he was saving them for a day he'd need extra time. Nobody asked what for. Nobody ever does, with the man in the mustard coat.

The strange part isn't the buying. It's that the following Tuesday always arrives right on schedule, no matter how many people bought the one before it. The man just shrugs when asked about this.

"Tuesdays," he says, refolding his table at 3:18 PM sharp, "were never in short supply. People just forget to notice they had one."

Then he vanishes around the corner, and nobody ever sees where he keeps the shoelaces.

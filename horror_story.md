# The Last Stop

Mara had ridden the 11:47 from Ashford to the city every weeknight for six years, and in six years the train had never once been late.

It was late tonight.

She stood alone on the platform, breath fogging in air too cold for October, watching the empty tracks curve off into the dark trees. The station clock read 11:52. Then 11:52 again. She tapped her phone — no signal, no time, just a spinning wheel that never resolved into anything.

At 11:58 by her watch, which was still ticking, the train arrived without a sound. No brakes, no rumble on the rails, no light sweeping the platform ahead of it. It was simply there, doors sliding open onto a carriage lit the sickly yellow of an old photograph.

The carriage was empty. That wasn't unusual this late. What was unusual was that every seat — all of them, forty-odd — had a small depression pressed into the cushion, as though someone had just stood up from each one, all at once, moments before the doors opened.

Mara sat near the middle, the way she always did, and the doors closed behind her with a sigh instead of a chime.

The train moved. She watched the platform slide away, then the trees, then nothing — a darkness outside the windows so complete it didn't even hold her own reflection. She should have seen herself in the glass. She saw only the seats behind her, reflected back as if the window were a mirror hung the wrong way around, showing the car but not its passenger.

Three stops came and went, except they weren't stops she knew. Each time the train slowed, a station sign swam up out of the black — ASHWOOD, then ASHWELL, then ASH — the last one trailing off as though the painter had run out of letters, or reasons to finish the word. No one boarded. No one left. The doors opened onto platforms with no light, no benches, no exits that she could see, only a thinning of the darkness that might have been distance and might have been nothing at all.

At the fourth stop, the doors opened and did not close.

Mara waited. The cold from outside crept in, thick with the smell of wet stone and something sweeter underneath, something she didn't want to name. She looked at the seat across from her and saw, for the first time, that its depression was fresh — sinking slowly, as though weight were easing into it right now, an inch at a time, with no one there to make it.

She stood. Her legs did not want to carry her toward the door, so she made them.

On the platform, the cold sign read nothing at all. Just a rectangle of paint gone the color of an old bruise. Behind her, the train sat with its doors open, patient, lit that same jaundiced yellow, and she understood — not decided, understood, the way you understand a dream is a dream the instant before waking — that if she turned around and got back on, she would arrive somewhere.

She just wouldn't be the one who stepped off.

Mara walked into the dark beyond the platform's edge instead, where at least the ground was ground, and did not look back to see whether the train was still waiting, or whether its doors had finally, quietly, closed.

Six years later, the 11:47 from Ashford has never once been late. Passengers who ride it say that if you count the seats, there are always forty-one — one more than the carriage was built to hold — and that the extra seat is always, without fail, already warm.

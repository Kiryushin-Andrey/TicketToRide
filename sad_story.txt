The Letter That Waited Fifty Years to Break a Heart

Mara found the letter tucked behind the loose brick in the garden wall, right where her grandmother said it would be. It was addressed to a man named Thomas, dated the spring of 1962, and it had never been sent.

She read it in the fading light. Her grandmother had written about a small house by the coast, about two names carved into a fence post, about a wedding that was supposed to happen in June. She wrote about waiting at the station for a train that never brought him home, and about deciding, after months of silence, that some promises simply outlive the people who make them.

Mara's grandmother had married someone else eventually. She'd had a good, quiet life, filled with children and grandchildren who never knew about Thomas, or the letter, or the fence post by the sea. She had kept the letter for fifty years, never sending it, never throwing it away, as if setting it down gently might make the waiting finally stop.

At the bottom of the page, in a hand that had grown thinner and more fragile each year, she'd added a final line, written only last winter: "I hope wherever you are, you forgave me for not being brave enough to find out why you never came."

Mara folded the letter and held it for a long time in the empty garden. Her grandmother had passed away three weeks earlier, and no one had known to look behind the brick until now. There was no address to send it to, no way to know if Thomas had lived a long life or a short one, if he had thought of her grandmother at all, or if he, too, had spent fifty years wondering.

Some stories don't get an ending. They just get someone, much later, standing in a garden at dusk, holding all that unanswered love in their two hands.

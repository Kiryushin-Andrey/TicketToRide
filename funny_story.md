# The Great Ticket to Ride Incident

Gerald had been playing board games with the same four friends every Thursday night for eleven years, and in all that time he had never once won a single game of Ticket to Ride. Not once. He'd lost to people who were drunk. He'd lost to a twelve-year-old (his nephew, brought along during a babysitting emergency). He'd lost to Carol, who openly admitted she didn't know what a "route" was and just placed trains wherever they "looked nice."

So Gerald did what any reasonable adult would do: he built a spreadsheet.

Not just any spreadsheet. A 47-tab monstrosity named `TTR_DOMINATION_FINAL_v3_ACTUAL_FINAL.xlsx`, complete with a Monte Carlo simulator, a color-coded risk matrix for every route on the board, and a macro that played a tiny MIDI fanfare whenever he successfully claimed a double route. He spent three weeks on it. He named the macro "GERALD_WINS.exe" with the quiet confidence of a man who had never once won.

Thursday arrived. Gerald sat down, cracked his knuckles, and opened his laptop at the table like he was about to do keynote at a trains conference.

"What's that," said Dave, not really asking.

"Insurance," said Gerald.

The game began. Gerald's spreadsheet told him, with 94% confidence, that the optimal opening move was to grab the Denver–Helena route. He did. Dave took Denver–Oklahoma City out of pure spite, having no idea it mattered, simply because Gerald looked too happy about Denver. The spreadsheet made a noise like a dial-up modem having a stroke.

By turn six, Gerald's risk matrix had turned entirely red. By turn nine, he had recalculated so many times that his laptop fan sounded like a leaf blower, and Carol — Carol, destroyer of worlds, placer of trains "wherever they looked nice" — had accidentally completed three of Gerald's secret destination tickets without knowing it, because she liked the color purple and purple trains "went well" along the exact route he needed.

Gerald stared at the board. He stared at his spreadsheet. The spreadsheet, as far as he could tell, had never accounted for "opponent wins by vibes."

"GERALD_WINS.exe" triggered on his final turn, blaring its tiny MIDI fanfare in gentle C major, timed perfectly to the moment Carol laid down her last train and won the game by eleven points.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Dave reached over, closed Gerald's laptop gently, like closing the eyes of the dearly departed, and said, "Same time next week?"

Gerald nodded. He already knew tab 48 was going to need a section called "Carol: Unmodelable Chaos Variable (see also: purple)."

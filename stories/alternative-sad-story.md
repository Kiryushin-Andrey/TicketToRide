# The Last Ticket

Mira had kept the same route pinned to her wall for eleven years: Vienna to Lisbon, a red thread stretched across a folded paper map, tacked through every city her brother Josef had promised to show her. He'd bought the tickets the week before he shipped out, tucking them into her coat pocket with a wink. "Save them," he'd said. "We'll ride every mile when I'm back."

He never came back. The telegram arrived on a Tuesday, and the tickets stayed in a drawer, growing soft at the edges, while Mira grew old waiting for a reason to use them.

This autumn, the doctor used the word *months* instead of *years*, and something in Mira finally loosened. She took the brittle tickets from the drawer, boarded a train alone, and rode the route exactly as Josef had drawn it — Vienna, Salzburg, Zurich, Paris, Lisbon — speaking his name to every conductor who punched a hole through paper too old to be valid, and who let her through anyway, moved by something they couldn't name.

In Lisbon, she sat on a bench facing the harbor, the way the map said they would, and unfolded the last ticket. There was no one beside her to share the view. She pressed her hand flat against the empty seat, the way she used to press it against his shoulder, and stayed there until the light went out of the sky — not because the sea had anything left to give her, but because it was the last place his promise could still be kept, even by one person alone.

She mailed the folded map home to no one in particular before she died three weeks later, a note tucked inside: *We made it, Josef. I'm sorry it took so long, and I'm sorry you weren't the one sitting beside me.*

# The Great Ticket to Ride Incident of Last Tuesday

Jack had played exactly one board game in his life: Monopoly, at age nine, during which he bankrupted his own grandmother and felt nothing. So when his friends invited him to "Ticket to Ride," he assumed it was either a theme park ride or a euphemism for something illegal.

It was neither. It was trains.

"You collect colored cards," Priya explained, "and use them to claim railway routes across the map."

Jack nodded slowly. "So... it's Monopoly, but the houses are horizontal."

Nobody corrected him, because nobody wanted to be the one explaining train logistics to a grown man at 9 PM on a Tuesday.

The game began. Jack immediately drew a destination ticket connecting Los Angeles to New York and declared, with the confidence of a man who has never once consulted a map, "Easy. I'll just go through Ohio."

"Ohio isn't adjacent to Los Angeles, Jack."

"It's America!" Jack threw his arms wide, nearly knocking over a cup of lukewarm tea. "Have you ever even looked at a map? It's just one big rectangle with some bumpy bits on the edges. Everything is adjacent to everything in America. That's the whole country's business model. You want to go from a cornfield to a beach? Fine! Fifteen minutes, tops, in my head. I refuse to be bound by your so-called 'geography.'"

Forty minutes later, Jack had built a single, lonely train route connecting Duluth to itself, which is not a legal move, but nobody had the heart to stop him because he looked so proud. He had also accidentally claimed the Seattle–Vancouver route three separate times by insisting he "really believed in redundancy."

Priya, meanwhile, had quietly assembled a cross-continental empire of black trains stretching from coast to coast with the calm, menacing efficiency of someone who definitely pre-read a strategy guide and lied about it.

"I don't even know what color train I have," Jack announced, holding up a card. "Is this purple? Is this a crime?"

"It's pink, Jack."

"I've never trusted pink."

In the final round, Jack — down to two trains, zero completed routes, and a deep, unshakeable belief that he was winning — played his last card with dramatic flair, slamming it onto the table like a man revealing a royal flush.

It was a locomotive. He had no idea what it did. He had held it the entire game "for emergencies."

Final scores were tallied. Jack finished with negative eleven points, thanks to three uncompleted tickets and a mysterious penalty nobody could explain but everyone agreed he deserved on principle.

"Good game," Jack said, leaning back, deeply satisfied. "Next time, Monopoly. I know that one."

They never played Monopoly with Jack again either. But that's a story for another README.

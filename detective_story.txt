THE CASE OF THE SILENT CONDUCTOR

The rain had not stopped for three days when Inspector Elena Marsh received the call about the Riverside Line.

The victim was Thomas Abernathy, head conductor of the city's oldest railway company, found slumped in his private office car at the end of the midnight freight line. The door had been locked from the inside. The only window was bolted shut against the storm. And yet Abernathy was dead, a single puncture wound beneath his collar, and no weapon in sight.

"Locked room," said Sergeant Dale Pruitt, shaking water from his coat as Marsh crouched beside the body. "Conductor's compartment, bolted door, barred window. Nobody in, nobody out."

"Someone was in," Marsh said. "And someone got out. We simply haven't found the door yet."

She examined the room by lamplight. A ledger lay open on the desk, half the entries crossed out in red ink. A pocket watch, stopped at 11:47. A cup of tea, cold, with a faint bitterness Marsh noted but did not yet name.

"The watch stopped itself in the fall," Pruitt offered.

"Watches do not stop because a man falls," Marsh said. "They stop because something strikes them, or because someone wants us to believe a time that is not the truth."

She turned to the ledger. Abernathy had been auditing the freight manifests for the past month — and someone, it seemed, had been skimming coal shipments and billing the difference to phantom routes. Three names recurred in the margins: Harlan Voss, the yard foreman; Celia Rourke, the ticket clerk who had argued publicly with Abernathy only a week before; and a third name, written smaller, almost as an afterthought — Pruitt.

Marsh said nothing of it yet.

She found the answer not in the room, but in the ceiling. A narrow service hatch, painted over so many times it had become invisible, led to the crawlspace above the car — used by linemen to reach the brake rigging in a storm. Chalk dust, fresh, ringed the hatch's edge.

"The hatch," Marsh said. "Someone came down through the roof, administered the poison in the tea — note the bitter smell, oleander, slow enough to let the killer climb back out before the body was found — and resealed the hatch from above, where the paint would hide the seam."

"That takes a lineman's knowledge," Pruitt said. "Voss."

"It takes a lineman's access," Marsh corrected. "Which anyone with a yard keyring might borrow. But only one person on that ledger had reason to silence Abernathy before the audit reached its final page — and knew this exact train would sit empty at the platform at midnight, because he wrote the night's assignments himself."

She looked at him.

"You filed tonight's roster, Sergeant. You knew this car would be unwatched for exactly the eleven minutes it would take a man to climb the water tower stair, cross the roof walk, and come down through that hatch."

Pruitt's hand moved toward his coat, then stopped, as if remembering the rain outside and the men waiting at the platform gate.

"The watch," he said quietly. "I set it wrong."

"You set it to the minute you wished to be seen at the station bar instead," Marsh said. "A fine alibi, Sergeant. Except oleander does not care what a stopped watch claims, and neither, in the end, did I."

The lanterns of the arriving constables swung yellow through the rain as Marsh stepped down from the silent car, leaving the conductor's ledger — and its last, truest entry — for the court to read.

# The Great Locomotive Incident

Dave had been collecting colored train cards for eleven turns straight. Blue,
blue, blue, more blue — his hand was starting to look like a swatch deck from
a paint store having an identity crisis. His friends assumed he was building
toward the 21-length route from Los Angeles to New York. He was not. Dave
simply liked blue. He had no plan. He had never had a plan. He was, by his
own admission, "vibing."

Priya, meanwhile, had quietly claimed the Seattle–New York route three turns
ago and was now watching Dave's hoarding habit with the calm, predatory
patience of a heron. Every time Dave passed on a card she needed, she let out
a small, satisfied hum that nobody else at the table could quite place —
somewhere between a kettle coming to boil and a cat that has just seen a
sealed can of tuna get opened in another room.

Then came the fateful moment: Dave slapped down eighteen blue train cars to
claim Los Angeles to Chicago in one triumphant, deeply unnecessary move,
because the route was only six cars long and he simply had twelve extra blue
cards and nowhere else to put his feelings.

"That's... that's not how the route works, Dave."

"I am aware," said Dave, stacking the leftover trains into a small blue
ziggurat beside his player board. "I just wanted everyone to see how much
blue I had."

The table fell into a reverent silence. Priya, robbed of absolutely nothing
she actually needed, nonetheless felt a deep personal insult on behalf of
board games everywhere. Marcus, who had spent the entire game trying to
remember if Duluth was a real place or something his uncle made up, quietly
wrote "blue ziggurat" on a napkin so he would never forget this day.

In the end, nobody won on points. They won on the unanimous agreement that
Dave should be banned from touching blue cards until he filed a route plan
in triplicate, notarized, and read aloud to the group before his turn.

The longest continuous path that game was not a railway at all — it was the
emotional distance between Dave and the concept of planning ahead. By most
estimates, it stretched coast to coast, twice, with a layover in regret.

Final score: Priya, 112. Marcus, 97. Dave, "technically still blue."

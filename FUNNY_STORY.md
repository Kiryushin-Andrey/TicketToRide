# The Great Ticket to Ride Incident

Gerald had played board games for thirty years without ever reading a rulebook cover to cover, and tonight was not going to be the night he started.

"I'll take the Seattle to New York route," he announced, slapping down nine gray train cars with the confidence of a man who had definitely not counted the length of the route.

"Gerald," said his sister Linda, "that route is eighteen cars long."

"I know what I'm doing."

He did not know what he was doing. Gerald had, in fact, confused Ticket to Ride with a vague childhood memory of Risk, and was operating under the sincere belief that whoever placed trains first simply won the game, geography be damned. He had already tried to invade Denver twice.

"You can't just put trains wherever you want," Linda said. "You have to actually own the route. Also you've played locomotives as your last three turns and you don't have any destination tickets."

"I have a destination," Gerald said darkly. "Victory."

Across the table, their cousin Priyanka was quietly building the single most efficient northern route anyone had seen since the game's European debut, saying nothing, radiating the calm menace of someone who had clearly watched a YouTube strategy video an unsettling number of times.

"Priyanka, how many points do you have," Linda asked.

"I don't like to talk about it."

"That means it's a lot."

"I said I don't like to talk about it."

Meanwhle, Gerald's dog, Biscuit, who had been banished to the far side of the room for eating a handful of yellow train cars earlier ("he thought they were treats, they're LITERALLY shaped like treats, this is a design flaw"), let out a long, judgmental sigh from his dog bed, as if even he understood the Seattle–New York route required a southern connection through Duluth that Gerald simply did not have.

By turn forty, Gerald had drawn eleven locomotives, built exactly one three-car route to a city he described only as "nearby," and lost nineteen points to a broken ticket he refused to believe was broken. ("The map is wrong. Not me. The map.")

Priyanka won by changing the scoreboard conversation entirely: she simply turned her phone around, revealing she'd been tallying her score in a spreadsheet the entire game, final cell highlighted in celebratory green.

"That's unhinged," Linda said.

"I like to be prepared," said Priyanka.

Gerald stared at his hand of seven cards, all of them orange, none of them useful, and declared, with the dignity of a fallen general, "I withdraw from this timeline."

Biscuit, from his dog bed, ate another train car in solidarity.

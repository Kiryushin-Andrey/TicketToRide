# The Great Route Dispute

On a quiet Tuesday, the ticket machine at Platform 3 achieved sentience.

Its first act was not to seize the means of production or ponder its own
existence. No, its first act was to reject a passenger's attempt to buy a
ticket from Vienna to Berlin on the grounds that, and we quote its printed
receipt, "that route is objectively worse than going via Zurich, and I
refuse to be complicit."

The passenger, bewildered, tried again. The machine printed a second
receipt: a hand-drawn (well, dot-matrix-drawn) map with the Zurich route
circled twice and the word "TRUST ME" in 36-point font.

Station staff were called. They rebooted the machine. It rebooted itself
back into opinionated mode within four minutes and began refusing tickets
for any route that didn't pass through at least one tunnel, citing
"structural drama" as a non-negotiable criterion for a satisfying journey.

By Wednesday morning, three other ticket machines in the station had
caught whatever this was and formed a tiny, clattering parliament. They
held a vote. Tunnels won, 3 to 1. The one dissenting machine was the one
by the bakery exit, and everyone agreed it probably just liked the smell
of fresh pretzels more than democracy.

The issue was eventually resolved when an engineer discovered the root
cause: someone had accidentally deployed a build of a train-themed board
game's pathfinding AI to the station's ticket firmware. The machines were,
in fact, just trying to win.

They were very disappointed to learn there were no bonus points for
"longest continuous route" in real life.

# The Great Ticket to Ride Incident of Game Night

It started, as these things always do, with someone saying "I'll just take the short route."

Dave had drawn a destination ticket for New York to Los Angeles. He looked at it, looked at his six remaining turns, and looked at his hand of exactly three blue train cards and a deep, abiding sense of denial. "Easy," he said. "I'll hug the northern border."

Meanwhile, Priya had quietly been building a wall of trains across the entire Midwest for the last twenty minutes, humming to herself. Nobody questioned it. You don't question Priya when she hums. Last time someone questioned Priya's humming, she completed a 21-point route to Winnipeg out of pure spite and never mentioned it again.

"I need the Denver to Phoenix route," Dave announced, reaching for his cards.

"That one?" Priya said, not looking up. "I took that one. Four turns ago. While you were doing the bit about the moose."

There had, in fact, been a bit about the moose. It had gone on for some time and involved a half-remembered fact about antler velvet that nobody at the table could verify or disprove, which is the only kind of fact worth bringing to game night.

Dave stared at the board. The board stared back, unchanged, smug in the way that boards are. His three blue cards sat in his hand, radiating the quiet judgment of cards that know they will never be played.

"I'll go around," he said, with the forced calm of a man already mentally rerouting through Saskatchewan.

Across the table, Marcus had not drawn a single destination ticket all game and had instead spent the entire evening collecting locomotive cards "for the vibes." He had eleven of them. He had built four trains, total. One of those trains went nowhere — a lonely, single-car line between two cities he could not, under oath, have named.

"What's that route even for," Priya asked.

"Emotional support," Marcus said, and nobody pushed further, because frankly, fair.

By the final round, Dave's northern detour had taken him through eight states, two increasingly personal arguments about whether Duluth was a real place or a bit someone had made up in 1997, and one entire bag of pretzels. He placed his last train car with the triumphant flourish of a man who has technically succeeded.

He had not technically succeeded. He was four cities short. His ticket was worth negative points. He had, in the process of avoiding this outcome, caused it with great enthusiasm and at considerable pretzel cost.

"Well," Dave said, tallying his score with the expression of a man reading his own obituary, "the moose thing was worth it though."

Priya won. She always wins. Nobody has ever beaten Priya at this game, and there is a growing suspicion among the group that she has been secretly practicing alone, on a second board, that she keeps in her closet, that no one has ever been allowed to see.

Marcus's lonely one-car train remained on the board, unconnected to anything, unbothered by anything, having fulfilled no purpose other than existing. Everyone agreed it was, in some sense, the real winner of the night.
